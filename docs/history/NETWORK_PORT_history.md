# Network Port: history

The tests, measurements and review notes of the chapters of [`NETWORK_PORT.md`](../NETWORK_PORT.md), kept in the order of the chapters there. The design and the rules are in that page; where the two disagree, that page is right.

Status: **the core, the room, a native TCP transport, the application integration and host migration are done and tested (v0.0.43 command layer and state hash, v0.0.44 protocol, sequencer, lock-step
runner and sessions, v0.0.45 room and start barrier and framed TCP, v0.0.46 host / join in the game, v0.0.47 host migration).** Open: NAT traversal is no longer planned inside the game (an external launcher or lobby provides the path between
players and starts the game with the addresses on its command line; raw TCP works on a LAN, a VPN or with a forwarded port, and v0.0.78 adds the discovery of rooms on the local network); a single-player game applies its commands at once.

A lost packet's resend that no buffer bridges is the same stall as before: as many of them a minute (2.4 against 2.1: the relay's spikes are the same number a second in every row) and each about 30 ms longer (the longest 220 ms against 187), since the buffer that would have held 100 ms of it holds 50; between them the delay is 84 ms lower. What the rule does not do is
buy a smooth game on a very lossy link at a low delay: a link whose turns are late by more than 150 ms in more than one turn in twenty gets the largest buffer (four turns, 200 ms) and stalls for the rest.

**What used to go wrong with the buffer** (found with the readout; each is reproduced by a test that fails without the fix, and all three now follow from the runner above):

1. *"Waiting for the other players..." stayed on the screen after a real stall.* The runner called itself stalled while its accumulator of unspent time (up to 400 ms after a stall) held a tick and no turn was
   queued; after a stall that is true for ever, although turns arrive on time. `NetGame::stalled_ms()` grew without end and the message never went. Now `stalled_ms()` is the time since a tick was due and had no
   turn, while that goes on: a turn that arrives and runs ends it at once. (Reproduced with a host and two guests, one of which draws no frame for 5 s: the host stops sealing after 3 s of missing acknowledgements, the
   other guest waits, and its count rose to 21 s. N9.19, N9.21.)

2. *A stall used the buffer up for good.* The 400 ms of unspent time were paid back against the turns that arrive afterwards, so every later turn ran the moment it arrived, with both its ticks at once: no buffer, the game
   stepping 10 times a second instead of 20, and every bit of jitter a visible stall (179 stalls over 100 ms in 40 s with jitter up to 60 ms). Now nothing is owed for a wait and the buffer is collected again (N9.22, N9.23, J2.1).

3. *A queue left standing was never run down.* A bunch of turns after a frozen link, a frame that took 400 ms, a window that is hardly drawn: the turns pile up, the runner ran at double speed only while more than
   five were queued and stayed at five for the rest of the match: half a second of delay for good, and two windows of one match running 0.5 s apart (the match clock shows it as 6:41 against 6:40). Now every queue
   longer than the buffer is run down at up to 4x, down to the buffer and 10 ms (N9.24, N9.25, N9.26, J2.3, J2.4).

Two more changes belong to the same cause. **The network's clock keeps real time**: `Application::run_frame_with_delta` clamps a frame to 100 ms for the local simulation, which also cut the network's clock; a hitch of 400 ms left
three turns standing for good. The network now gets the real frame time up to one second (N5.30). **A long frame runs the ticks that its time stands for** (above; N9.28): a window that is drawn once in 0.75 s (the built-in browser pane of a
desktop tool draws a hidden page at about one frame a second: measured 1.3; **Chrome draws a hidden tab not at all**, see "A hidden tab" below, which since v0.0.96 the server's messages run all the same) used to run 8 of the 15 ticks that its time holds, so its game ran at 40% of the speed, and the host, which did not seal more than 30 turns ahead of the slowest peer, held
every other player's game back to that speed.

**The server's turn clock.** `ants_server` seals by a schedule of its own (`next_seal_ms += kTurnMs`, not "50 ms after the pass"), so the turns cannot drift or bunch; the loop sleeps 2 ms a pass, so each turn goes
out when the first pass after its time runs. Measured with a client that polled without sleeping: in Docker (Linux, through the published port) the turns reached a client that polled without sleeping every **50.00 ms, sd 1.11 ms, 47.7 - 53.0 ms over 740 turns** (natively on macOS: sd 1.16, 47.6 - 53.7); with 12 rooms of 4 clients playing, at the 48 clients: sd 1.56 ms, 1st to 99th percentile 46.4 - 53.5, **worst 56.9 ms, none bunched, none later than 7 ms from its place**. The mean is exact (no drift); the spread is the pass: a turn waits for the first pass after its time, uniformly 0 - 2.2 ms, sd 1.1. Nothing to fix. The server of v0.0.91 (turns of 100 ms, built from
the base commit, the same stack and the same clients) gave **100.00 ms, sd 1.12 ms, 97.5 - 102.8 ms over 371 turns**, and with the 12 rooms sd 1.51 ms, worst 109.8 ms: the spread in milliseconds is the same before and after (the code of the clock is unchanged), so it is twice as large a
share of a turn now (2.2 % against 1.1 %) and still far below a frame (16.7 ms). A variant that slept until the next turn was due (`sleep_for` the remaining microseconds, at most 2 ms) was tried and
dropped: on macOS the timer wakes late and unevenly (sd 4.2 ms, worst 79 ms against 1.2 and 54), on Linux the fixed pass is already within a millisecond.

**The server's load.** 12 demo rooms of 4 headless clients (`room_load`: Hello, loaded, a turn read and acknowledged per poll, a ping a second, a `Hatch` every 700 ms; the match runs on the referee's engine) at 50 ms turns: **the server container used 8.5 % of one core on average (6.2 - 12.8 % over 32 samples; 1.4 % idle: the 2 ms pass) and 3 - 4 MB of memory**, the turns reached all 48 clients 50.0 ms apart (above), and nobody was ever called a lagger or dropped (64,320 turns read). Natively on macOS the same load took 2.4 % of a core.
**Before, the same measurement against the server of v0.0.91 (turns of 100 ms; the same stack, the same clients, `docker stats` once a second for 32 s): 5.7 % of one core on average (4.4 - 7.8 %; 1.4 % idle; 3.0 - 4.0 MB), the turns 100.0 ms apart, 32,175 read.** Halving the turn costs 1.7 times the work above the idle pass (7.1
points of a core against 4.3, 2.8 points more for 48 players: the simulation is the same 20 ticks a second, the turns, the acknowledgements and the writes are twice as many), about 0.6 % of a core per room. The load is one thread: by extrapolation (not tested) a core carries more than a hundred rooms
before a pass takes longer than a turn.
**The number of demo rooms** that the stack allows (`ANTS_DEMO_ROOMS`) is 48 by default (12 until v0.8.1, the size of the measurement above). The cap is what stops a script from filling the server through a page that has no secret: a room that is still waiting for players keeps its place for up to ten minutes, and only a running match that all its players left a minute ago gives its place up when none is free. By the 0.6 percent of a core per room above, 48 busy rooms cost about 30 percent of one core, plus one `fsync` a second each on the same thread (1.35 ms on average in a Linux docker VM, S3.100: about 65 ms of every second). These are estimates: nothing was measured on the production machine, and what counts is the speed of one core and of its disk, not the number of cores. The stack starts the server with `--log-mb 4` so that the demo rooms cannot use up the 256 MiB that all the turn logs share (at 16 MiB a room, 16 hostile rooms could; a busy 30-minute match logs 0.65 MB): keep the rooms times `--log-mb` under 256 (`tests/scripts/test_default_map.py` checks it).

## Reconnect (protocol 10): a player whose connection is lost can come back

A request: when a player disconnects, the player should get some time to reconnect, and after some time the other players might be asked whether they want to continue without that player; after a browser crash or a power cut, the player could communicate with the other players, who could hold the game paused until the player is back. The original has no pause, no vote and no way back (5.47 of `docs/GAME_REVERSE_ENGINEERING.md`: a peer that is gone is dropped, the others read "%s dropped out of the game!"). **Release A** (protocol 10, the server side behind a switch: a room holds seats only when it is told to, so nothing changes for players) **shipped in v0.0.98**; **release B** adds the clients and turns it on: **`NetGame`'s part is built** (the library comes back to its match by itself: "What the clients do in release B"), the application's screens and the key storage are built too (the same section), and **the switch is on**: `kReconnectByDefault` is true, so every room of a server holds seats unless the server is started with `--no-reconnect` or the room says `"reconnect": false`. This section says what exists and what is left.

**Restart records:** the server also keeps the matches of its rooms across a restart of the server itself: see "Restart records" below (no new protocol number; `NetGame` rejoins by itself and the application shows it). **Status: the server side is complete, and on by default since release B** (`--no-reconnect` turns it off for a server, a room's `"reconnect": false` for one room). What is in: protocol 10 (the messages below), `TurnLog` (the server's log of every turn, bounded per room and by a budget that all rooms share), `Attendance` (the rules of the pause, the vote and the cap), `LockstepRunner::fast_forward` (the catch-up without a hook, the jitter buffer left alone), the lobby's keys, **`HostSession`** (holds the seat of a lost connection and pauses the match, tells the players who is missing, takes a returning connection over, streams the log to it, compares its state hash with the referee's and gives the seat back), **`ClientSession`** (a lost link is not the end: new link, Hello with the key, the match again, the server's word; a Reject ends it), and the server around them: the room's keys and settings, the door that takes a Hello with a key to the running room, the status with its counters, the control interface's `reconnect`, `hold_vote_seconds`, `max_pause_seconds`, `max_catch_up_seconds` and `resume_countdown_seconds`, the options `--reconnect`, `--no-reconnect`, `--hold-vote-seconds`, `--max-pause-seconds`, `--max-catch-up-seconds`, `--resume-countdown-seconds` and `--log-mb`, and the budget for the logs. **After the independent review of release A** (its findings are one section below, "What a key holder can do to a room") a seat holder has budgets (a catch-up time per absence, three Hellos a minute, three times the log's size in ten minutes), the pause cap is absolute, a seat that flaps is put to the vote, the clients' runners forget a pause, a log that is dead frees its memory at once, a client gives up by the room's cap, and the match is held for a **resume countdown** after a pause. **The game's own clients** were not in release A: `NetGame` now uses `ClientSession`'s reconnect when a room hands it a key ("What the clients do in release B", below); the application's screens and the key storage came with release B, which turned the switch on. Everything about LAN and direct games is unchanged.

## Restart records: a running match survives a restart of the server

### Restoring

**The server serves while it restores.** The loop runs while the queue is replayed: S3.115 replays a record of an hour of turns and one of the busiest map's four players while another match is made and played, and no pass takes more than its slice and the 10 - 18 ms that the first piece of a room needs (the match that is played meanwhile keeps its 50 ms turns); the `reconnect` part of `tests/scripts/test_ants_server.sh` restores a thousand records for seconds while `/busy` and a new room's Hello are answered at once. What is not sliced: judging the records at the start reads each file whole (about 0.2 s for a record of 38 MiB), and so does the first piece of its room.

### Measured (S3.100; macOS, Apple silicon, Release build, on a machine that shared its cores with other work: the load average was about 40 on 10 cores, so these are upper bounds, not a quiet machine's best)

* **A record's size**: a minute of play adds **21.7 KB** when nobody gives an order (1,200 turns of 17 bytes and 60 checkpoints), **25.9 KB** for three players who each give an order every 0.7 s and **27.4 KB** for four (the machines' orders are random group moves: busy play). A match of 11 minutes 40 s on TREASURE with four players (13,999 turns, the test's longest): **350 KiB**; a 12-minute match is about 14,400 turns and 360 KiB.

* **The cost of writing**: **2.1 - 2.6 microseconds a turn** (100,000 turns in 0.21 - 0.26 s: one `write()` of about 25 bytes); a flush of a second's turns costs **0.04 ms on average and 0.5 - 0.6 ms at the worst** (macOS's `fsync` does not flush the drive's own cache). The same test on Linux (Debian 12, GCC 12, inside a docker VM, real `fsync` on its overlay file system): **0.56 microseconds a turn, 1.35 ms on average and 6.6 ms at the worst for a flush**. The room does one a second, spread over the second by a hash of the code, and they all run on the server's one thread, so 256 rooms cost 256 times the average: macOS 0.04 ms x 256 is about **10 ms of every second**; the Linux VM 1.35 ms x 256 is about **350 ms, a third of the thread**; a volume at 30 ms a flush x 256 would be **more than a second a second** at one flush a second, so the rooms that find it slow ask less often (their intervals double up to 10 s, L6): about **0.77 s of every second**, and a power cut can take up to ten seconds of turns. A flusher thread (the `write()` stays on the thread, only the `fsync` moves) is phase 2 material.

* **Bringing a match back**: TINY with three players, 5 minutes of play (5,599 turns): **0.10 s**; **TREASURE with four players, 11 minutes 40 s of play (13,999 turns: the engine's busiest map and the longest match): 2.3 - 2.9 s** (4,800 - 6,000 turns a second; a full 12-minute match is 14,400 turns, so about 2.5 - 3 s). The server serves while it restores (the replay runs in slices of 5 ms a pass: S3.115; the longest pass of the 13,599-turn match took 17.5 ms). Busy play replays at about 4,200 turns a second here, so the cap of 20 s of work is about 80,000 turns of four players' play (extrapolated): a longer match is refused as too slow. (The same replay under AddressSanitizer + UBSan on an unoptimised build takes minutes: the suites' bounds are for the Release build and the sanitizer runs print the numbers without the bounds' failing.)

* **Stopping**: the real program exits **2 ms** after SIGTERM on macOS and 4 ms on Linux (S3.95), `docker stop` of the container took **0.18 - 0.23 s** (exit code 0; docker's own round trip is most of it), `docker stop` of the stack as it was then (no `--reconnect`, which was the default: nothing to keep) 0.19 s.
